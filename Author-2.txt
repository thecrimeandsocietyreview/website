Abstract 
Background: The prevention of  terrorism and cyberterrorism increasingly depends on the 
coordination of  security institutions, intelligence capabilities and digital resilience. Global 
interdependence and the use of  cyberspace complicate the distinction between internal and 
external threats, while predictive technologies raise questions about legality, evidence and 
accountability. 
Purpose: This article examines how a National Security Strategy can integrate the 
prevention of  terrorism and cyberterrorism while preserving the rule of  law and 
fundamental rights. It argues that effective prevention requires institutional coordination 
without collapsing the distinct functions of  intelligence, cybersecurity, national defence, 
criminal investigation and judicial review. 
Methodology: The article adopts a legal-dogmatic and interdisciplinary approach. It 
analyses the Portuguese constitutional and statutory framework, relevant national and 
European strategic instruments, and selected scholarship in security studies, intelligence 
studies and crime prevention. The analysis distinguishes threat, risk, intelligence and 
evidence and assesses preventive measures against legal basis, institutional competence, 
evidential status, necessity, proportionality and accountability. 
Findings and implications: Intelligence-led policing, predictive analytics and artificial 
intelligence can improve early warning and prioritisation, but their outputs remain 
probabilistic and cannot independently establish criminal responsibility or justify coercive 
measures. The article proposes a boundary-oriented model in which information-sharing 
and operational cooperation are paired with clear mandates, meaningful human oversight, 
data-protection safeguards and reviewable decisions. It concludes that a coherent national-
security strategy should connect internal security, defence, intelligence and cybersecurity 
while preserving their legal and institutional distinctions. This approach can strengthen 
preparedness in strategic planning and operational practice without turning preventive 
security into indiscriminate surveillance or treating algorithmic assessments as evidence. 
Keywords: Terrorism, Cyberterrorism, National Security, Prevention; Intelligence, 
Cybersecurity, Fundamental Rights, Artificial Intelligence

The prevention of  terrorism and cyberterrorism poses a rule-of-law challenge involving 
legal, institutional, technological and strategic dimensions. Globalization, infrastructure 
interdependence and digitalization have blurred the distinction between internal and 
external threats. In cyberspace, actors can operate across borders and produce effects in 
several States without physical movement. 
The article asks how a National Security Strategy can organize the prevention of  terrorism 
and cyberterrorism without compromising the rule of  law. It argues for coordination 
among intelligence, internal security, cybersecurity, national defence, criminal investigation 
and international partners, while preserving their distinct mandates. Information-sharing 
and predictive tools may improve early warning, but probabilistic outputs are not evidence 
and cannot replace legal authority, human judgement or review. 
Methodologically, the article adopts a legal-dogmatic and interdisciplinary approach, 
analysing Portuguese constitutional and statutory rules, relevant national and European 
strategic instruments, and selected scholarship in security, intelligence and crime-prevention 
studies. Sources were purposively selected for their relevance to the institutional 
architecture and legal limits of  prevention; this is neither a systematic literature review nor 
an empirical evaluation of  operational effectiveness. The analysis assesses preventive 
capabilities by operational relevance, legal basis, institutional competence, evidential status, 
proportionality and accountability. It is limited to Portugal, using European and 
comparative material where directly relevant. 
The article’s contribution is to formulate a boundary-oriented model for preventive 
security: cooperation and information-sharing should be strengthened, but intelligence, 
criminal investigation, cybersecurity, cyber-defence and judicial decision-making must retain 
distinct mandates and evidential standards. The model evaluates preventive capabilities 
through six connected questions: operational relevance, legal basis, institutional 
competence, evidential status, proportionality and accountability. The article first examines 
Portugal’s security architecture, then analyses terrorism, cyberterrorism and preventive 
technologies, and finally identifies the legal safeguards required for rights-compatible 
prevention.

Recent geopolitical tensions, including Russia-NATO confrontation and renewed strategic 
competition, contribute to an uncertain security environment. Such developments matter 
to national planning, but should be assessed alongside transnational terrorism, cyber threats 
and domestic vulnerabilities rather than treated as a substitute for risk analysis. 41
The foregoing calls for a broader theorization of  national security. 
Realist approaches traditionally define national security through territorial integrity, 
sovereignty and protection against external threats, while assigning internal security a role 
in maintaining public order and protecting institutions. Contemporary interdependence 
makes this division incomplete: cyber operations, transnational networks and critical-
infrastructure dependencies can produce effects across both domains. A national-security 
strategy should therefore integrate these dimensions without erasing their distinct legal 
mandates. National security can be analysed through distinct but connected functions: 
national defence and military security; intelligence concerning threats to State interests; 
internal security and law enforcement; and human security, including civil protection and 
the protection of  people. These functions overlap in practice but remain subject to 
different legal mandates and accountability mechanisms. An integrated strategy should 
coordinate them without merging their competences. Beck’s account of  the “risk society” 
helps explain how technological, environmental and transnational risks cross borders and 
expose local vulnerabilities. This perspective supports a multidimensional approach to 
national security rather than a narrow focus on military threats. 42
In 2013, the Strategic Concept of  National Defence (SCND) was approved. It defined “the 
fundamental aspects of  the overall strategy to be adopted by the State for the achievement 
of  the objectives of  national security and defence policy”. The SCND refers expressly to 43
the concept of  national security, although the previous 2003 SCND had already addressed 
it, albeit less explicitly. The current SCND approaches the ISL as an integral part of  a 
broader and more comprehensive National Security system, advocating cooperation 
between the Armed Forces (AF) and the Security Forces and Services (SFS) in combating 
transnational threats. In this respect, it is important to emphasise that the SCND is 
provided for in Article 7 of  Law No. 31-A/2009 of  7 July and that, under paragraph 1 of  
that provision, it defines “the State’s priorities in the field of  defence, according to the 
national interest” and forms an integral part of  national defence policy. Accordingly, it 
concerns State priorities in the field of  defence rather than the domain of  internal security.

Several European states, including Spain, France and the United Kingdom, have adopted 
national-security strategies that coordinate public institutions and, where relevant, private 
actors. These examples provide comparative context for considering an integrated 
Portuguese framework.  There is therefore a case for developing a National Security 
Strategy that encompasses internal security and national defence, while remaining aligned 
with the applicable European security strategy. Moreover, any such strategy must respond 
to contemporary challenges, which are constantly changing. For the purposes of  this 
article, the expression “National Security Strategy” is used as a proposed integrating 
strategic framework rather than as a claim that Portugal currently possesses a single 
comprehensive statutory strategy bearing that exact institutional status. The argument is 
therefore normative and architectural: it concerns how the relevant security, defence, 
intelligence and cybersecurity instruments could be coordinated within a coherent strategic 
framework, while remaining subject to the competences and legal limits established by the 
Constitution and ordinary legislation. Automated analysis of  biographical, image, voice and 
other identifying data can assist counter-terrorism work where information volumes exceed 
manual capacity. Its use nevertheless requires a lawful purpose, reliable provenance, access 
controls and safeguards against unjustified interference with rights. Automation may 
improve the processing of  large datasets, but it does not remove the need for human 
assessment, necessity and proportionality. Analytical outputs should inform, not 
independently determine, preventive or coercive decisions. Any national strategy 
concerning internal security should therefore incorporate and remain coherent with 
relevant European and national strategies. In particular, it should be consistent with the 
European Union Internal Security Strategy, the EU strategy for the eradication of  
trafficking in human beings, the EU drugs strategy, the EU cybersecurity strategy, and the 
EU strategy for combating terrorist radicalization and recruitment. 44

1.1.3. The New Security Paradigm 
The contemporary national-security paradigm combines policy coordination, legal 
measures and international cooperation. It reflects the fact that domestic security decisions 
may affect regional and global peace and security. 
Its development may therefore be structured around four pillars that reinforce the notion 
of  National Security: “National Defence and the Armed Forces; Internal Security and the 
Police Forces; State Security and the Intelligence Services; Community Security and Civil 
Protection”. Beck’s “risk society” highlights how technological and urban development 59
coexist with complex risks that cross political, economic and social boundaries. Security 
analysis must therefore account for multidimensional vulnerabilities rather than military 
threats alone. Contemporary risks are networked, adaptable and capable of  affecting 60
individual well-being and security at local, national and international levels. 61
States consequently need responses that are dynamic, coordinated, integrated and 
multidisciplinary. 62
Security policy now extends beyond military threats to include crises such as natural 
disasters, pandemics and food insecurity, whose effects may undermine social resilience. 63
Portugal’s position at the European Union’s external border gives internal-security policy a 
clear European dimension. National measures must therefore align with EU cooperation in 
the Area of  Freedom, Security and Justice and with relevant common strategies, while 
reflecting Portugal’s own constitutional allocation of  responsibilities. This reinforces the 
case for a national-security framework that connects internal security, defence, intelligence 
and cybersecurity, rather than treating them as isolated policy fields. Portugal’s EU 
membership means that national security choices also affect European cooperation and 
shared security objectives.

2.1. Contextual Analysis 
The geopolitical scenario is increasingly uncertain and complex, making it possible to 
identify “multiple and different global threats which, directly and/or indirectly, affect 
Portugal and Portuguese interests abroad, (…) [in particular] the terrorist threat of  Islamist

Globalization has made terrorism more transnational, although its organisation, targets and 
operational reach may remain local or regional. Definitions vary, but recurring features 
include politically motivated violence, attacks on civilians or non-combatants, and the 
pursuit of  ideological or religious objectives. 75

Alongside terrorism, hybrid threats are another category that must be considered in 
everyday security because of  their increasing presence and complexity. The European 
Centre of  Excellence for Countering Hybrid Threats describes hybrid threats as 
coordinated combinations of  state and non-state activity, conventional and unconventional 
methods, and military and non-military instruments. They may exploit legal, political, 
economic, informational and technological vulnerabilities while remaining below the 
threshold of  open armed conflict. Their prevention requires cross-sectoral assessment and 
cooperation, but responses must remain proportionate and grounded in reliable attribution. 
Hybrid threats combine conventional and unconventional, military and non-military 
instruments, often using cyber operations, information campaigns and economic or 
political pressure to achieve strategic objectives. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed how 78
information and disinformation operations can exploit institutional pressure, weaken public 
confidence and distort government decision-making. Terrorism can exploit the openness 79
and trust that sustain democratic societies. This insight matters for prevention because 
excessive or indiscriminate security measures may undermine social cohesion and public 
confidence themselves. Counter-terrorism policy should therefore combine the protection 
of  potential targets with careful differentiation between lawful conduct, indicators of  risk 
and evidence of  criminal activity. 
The Paris and Copenhagen Schools offer critical perspectives on how security institutions 
define threats and justify preventive action. The Paris School examines security routines 
and technologies in everyday governance; the Copenhagen School analyses how issues are 
framed as existential threats. Both underline that threat definition and response are political 
and institutional choices requiring scrutiny. Contemporary security therefore includes 
economic, environmental and societal dimensions in addition to political and military 
security. 
2.2. Terrorism 
Contrary to a widespread perception, terrorism should not simply be regarded as a global 
phenomenon. It may instead be understood as a limited and circumscribed phenomenon 
and as an instrument used by a political, state or clandestine organisation to pursue political 
objectives in a regional or local context.

For analytical and legal purposes, cyberterrorism should be distinguished from cybercrime, 
hacktivism, cyber espionage and state-sponsored cyber operations, even where methods or 
infrastructure overlap. The use of  digital tools, the disruption of  a network or the political 
character of  an online act is not, by itself, sufficient to establish a terrorist offence. 
Classification requires assessment of  conduct, intent, target, consequences and applicable 
statutory elements. This distinction matters operationally: threat intelligence may justify 
further assessment or protective action, but coercive measures and criminal attribution 
require an adequate legal basis and evidence appropriate to the decision being taken. 80
2.3. Intelligence in the Prevention of  Terrorism and Cyberterrorism 
Terrorism is one of  the principal threats to the security of  States and citizens. The struggle 
takes place on several fronts: combating ideology, dismantling support structures for 
terrorist groups and, equally importantly, disrupting the financial flows that sustain terrorist 
activities. 
2.3.1. Intelligence and the Cyber Domain 
Information is a vital resource for modern States, and intelligence increasingly contributes 
to information superiority through an improved understanding of  the cyber domain. Such 
superiority may translate into decision-making advantages and strategic and tactical 
benefits. It depends, among other factors, on cyber power, the scale and complexity of  
attacks, the robustness of  defence, policy positions, systemic vulnerabilities and 
dependencies, and the anonymity and attribution problems associated with cyber actors. 
2.3.2. Intelligence, Investigation and Institutional Coordination 
The proactive nature of  cyberterrorism prevention makes intelligence production and early 
warning particularly relevant. This prospective dimension does not, however, confer 
exclusive preventive responsibility on intelligence services. Prevention and response depend 
on coordination among competent authorities within their respective legal mandates.

Such integration does not, however, eliminate the functional boundaries between the 
different security systems. Intelligence has an essentially prospective and anticipatory 
function; criminal investigation seeks to establish facts, identify offenders and obtain 
evidence; cybersecurity and cyber-defence, according to their respective legal and 
institutional frameworks, protect and enhance the resilience of  systems and infrastructures; 
and judicial decision-making retains its constitutional autonomy. Cooperation must 
therefore coexist with a clear differentiation of  competences. 
Accordingly, the preventive use of  analytical technologies should be assessed through a 
cumulative sequence: whether the capability is technically and operationally relevant; 
whether there is a sufficient legal basis for its use; which institution is competent to act; 
what evidential status the resulting information has; whether the contemplated measure is 
necessary, suitable and proportionate; and whether the decision remains subject to 
meaningful institutional and legal accountability. 
In the field of  terrorism and cyberterrorism, this distinction is particularly relevant. Not 
every cyberattack constitutes cyberterrorism, just as the use of  digital means by a terrorist 
organisation does not automatically transform every digital activity into an act of  
cyberterrorism. Legal classification depends on the material and subjective elements 
required by the applicable legal regime. Prevention must also distinguish threat, risk, intent, 
preparation and evidence, avoiding the conversion of  prospective assessments into 
individual imputations without additional grounds. 
The use of  predictive policing, intelligence-led policing and artificial intelligence may 
strengthen analytical and anticipatory capacity, but it does not eliminate uncertainty or 
replace legally responsible human decision-making. Probabilistic risk estimates do not 
amount to criminal evidence, and statistical correlations do not, in themselves, establish 
individual authorship. The use of  these tools must comply with legality, necessity, suitability 
and proportionality, as well as applicable requirements concerning data protection, human 
oversight, transparency and accountability. 
At institutional level, effective prevention depends on coherence among national 
instruments relating to internal security, national defence, intelligence, cybersecurity and 
cyber-defence. The current legal framework reinforces the need for coordination and 
resilience without eliminating the functional autonomy of  the entities involved. The 
objective should not be to concentrate all preventive capabilities in a single entity, but to 
build cooperation mechanisms capable of  sharing relevant information, reducing 
vulnerabilities and ensuring proportionate responses to different categories of  threat. 
Effective prevention integrates information and action while preserving legality, 
proportionality, evidence and accountability. A strategy should improve cooperation 
without treating intelligence assessments as proof  or allowing technology to determine 
coercive decisions.
