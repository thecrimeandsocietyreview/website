Abstract 
Prison advocacy in the twenty-first century represents a paradigmatic shift from punitive incarceration 
to constitutional correction, human dignity, and social reintegration. In India, the prison system remains 
overburdened with an occupancy rate of  131.4% and under trial prisoners constituting 75.8% of  total 
inmates (National Crime Records Bureau [NCRB], 2022). This structural crisis exposes deep fissures 
between constitutional guarantees under Articles 14, 19, 21 and 39A and ground realities of  access to 
justice. The present article, situated within the theme of  The Crime & Society Review – Rethinking 
Crime, Justice & Society, critically examines the historical evolution of  prison reforms, contemporary 
challenges of  overcrowding, legal aid deficits, and socio-legal exclusion of  vulnerable groups including 
women, children, persons with mental illness, and Scheduled Castes and Tribes. Drawing upon 
doctrinal and empirical analysis, the paper conceptualizes prison advocacy as a rights-based, multi-
stakeholder intervention encompassing free legal aid, timely bail facilitation under Section 479 of  
Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) 2023, legal literacy, psychosocial support including stress 
and anger management, and rehabilitation planning. The article presents a detailed case study of  the 
Prison Advocacy Project at National Law University Odisha (NLUO), Cuttack, launched in 2024 in 
collaboration with Azim Premji Foundation, which has within one year interacted with 881 under trial 
prisoners, secured bail for 90 prisoners (60 free of  cost), provided legal assistance in 28 cases, and 
conducted 52 legal awareness sessions covering 13 topics benefiting over 2000 inmates across 
Choudwar Circle Jail, Banki Sub-Jail, Bhubaneswar Special Jail and Khordha District Jail. The Banki 
Sub-Jail witnessed significant reduction in population, demonstrating efficacy of  timely legal 
intervention. The paper argues that such academic-civil society-state partnerships must be 
institutionalized nationally as part of  Sustainable Development Goal 16 and Digital Social Citizenship 
framework. 
Keywords: Prison Advocacy, Under trial Prisoners, Legal Aid, Criminal Justice Reform, Rehabilitation, 
NLUO, Article 21, BNSS, SDG 16, Digital Social Citizenship

2. Conceptual Framework 
Four theories of  punishment: deterrent, retributive, preventive, reformative (Paranjape, 2019). Ancient 
India emphasized deterrence – Manusmriti recommended prisons along public roads so punishment 
visible. Colonial regime institutionalized deterrence via Prisons Act 1894. Post-independence, 
reformative theory gained primacy, influenced by international norms like UN Standard Minimum 
Rules for Treatment of  Prisoners (Nelson Mandela Rules, 2015). Coyle (2002) argues prison 
management must be based on human rights, not merely security. Petersilia (2003) highlights 
importance of  re-entry planning – housing, employment, family acceptance – to reduce recidivism.

3. Historical Evolution 
Ancient and Medieval: Ramayana mentions Lord Rama enquiring about state of  prisons in Ayodhya. 
Mahabharata refers to karagriha and one of  18 state officials as head of  prisons. Manusmriti 
recommends deterrent construction. Pre-Buddhist era infamous for cruelty – daily whippings, shackles, 
forced labour. Fa-Hien and Hiuen Tsang documented Emperor Ashoka's Naraka hellish prisons before 
conversion. Under Muslim rule, forts converted into prisons, relatively milder.Colonial: 1805 H.S. 
Strachey surveyed Company territories documenting 43 civil, 75 criminal, 68 mixed jails (Government 
of  India, 1838). Macaulay Report 1835 – after murder of  Magistrate Richardson, Superintendent 
Calcutta Jail, Lord Macaulay emphasized best criminal code ineffective without sound punishment 
mechanism – recommended abolition outdoor labour, indoor work, classification, inspectors, central 
jails. Prison Discipline Committee 1836-38 chaired by H. Shakespeare reported 1838, exposed 
corruption, recommended separation undertrial-convicted, IG Prisons. Commission on Jail 
Management and Discipline 1864 – minimum space 54 sq ft and 640 cubic ft, sanitation, diet, medical, 
gender-age separation. Calcutta Conference 1877 – unified framework. Fourth Jail Commission 1888 
Lord Dufferin – echoed need consolidated Prisons Act – leading to Prisons Act 1894.Post-
Independence: Indian Jail Committee 1919-20 landmark – shift deterrence to reformation, trained staff, 
juvenile justice, opposed child incarceration with adults. Government of  India Act 1935 – prisons state 
subject. Reckless Commission 1952 – UN support Dr. W .C. Reckless – jails as reformative centres, 
juvenile courts, revision manuals, alternatives short imprisonment. Model Prison Manual 1960 – 
scientific correctional treatment. Mulla Committee 1980-83 – magnum opus – national prison policy, 
human rights, legal aid, living conditions, women, open prisons, aftercare. 
4. Constitutional And Legal Safeguards 
Sunil Batra (1978, 1980) – prisoners retain fundamental rights. Article 21 includes dignity, health, 
speedy trial, free legal aid (Hussainara Khatoon 1979). Article 39A obligation for free legal aid. Section 
479 BNSS 2023 – mandates release of  undertrials completing one-third for first-time offenders or one-
half  of  maximum sentence. Supreme Court in Re: Inhuman Conditions in 1382 Prisons (W .P . 
406/2013) order 23 Oct 2024 directed retrospective application of  Section 479, jail superintendents to 
identify eligible women and undertrials, mandatory reporting. Model Prison Manual 2016 Rule 5.47 
orientation programme, Rule 5.35 handbook rights. Early Access to Justice – police stations must 
display legal aid contact, inform arrestee rights, leaflet (D.K. Basu v. State of  West Bengal, 1997). At 
police station – rights from interrogation, consult advocate, grounds of  arrest, bail rights, leaflet, 
intimation to Legal Aid Committee. At prison – display rights, orientation, legal aid clinic, Undertrial 
Review Committee weekly.

6. Emergence Of  Prison Advocacy 
Prison advocacy is organized intervention to ensure dignity, accountability, reintegration. As Justice P .N. 
Bhagwati observed, "Legal aid is not charity, but a necessary adjunct to administration of  
justice" (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979). 
7. Case Study: Prison Advocacy Project NLUO-A 21St Century Model 
Prison advocacy is defined as organized efforts by legal professionals, academicians, civil society, and 
community leaders to protect prisoners' rights, ensure access to justice, promote humane treatment, 
and facilitate reintegration (Coyle, 2002). In the 21st century paradigm, it moves beyond welfare to 
rights-based empowerment. 
Advocacy encompasses:- Free, competent legal representation from arrest to acquittal; Facilitation of  
bail under Section 479 BNSS; Legal literacy and rights awareness; Psychosocial support — stress and 
anger management; Rehabilitation and reintegration planning .As Justice P .N. Bhagwati observed in 
Hussainara Khatoon (1979), "Legal aid is not charity, but a necessary adjunct to administration of  
justice." 
Genesis-On 10th December 2020, coinciding with International Human Rights Day, Prison 
Establishment Day was observed for first time in Odisha, symbolizing shift from punitive to 
rehabilitative justice. Recognizing acute under trial crisis, National Law University Odisha (NLUO), 
Cuttack, in collaboration with Azim Premji Foundation, launched Prison Advocacy Project in 2024, 
piloted from February 2025. 
Objectives and Scope-The project seeks to address socio-legal needs of  vulnerable prisoners through: 
Providing free legal aid ensuring competent representation; Facilitating timely bail through efficient 
procedures; Promoting rehabilitation via counselling, education, skill development; Conducting 
monthly sensitization workshops on prisoners' rights Strengthening research and advocacy for systemic 
reforms. Initially piloted in four prisons — Circle Jail Choudwar, Sub-Jail Banki, Special Jail 
Bhubaneswar, and District Jail Khordha — the project has comprehensive mission (NLUO, 2025).

Case Study 1: Samir Naik – Overcoming Prolonged Detention through Legal Intervention and 
Social Support 
Background: Samir Naik, aged 31, a resident of  Alasua village, Cuttack district, was 
arrested on 23 June 2024 in connection with multiple theft-related cases. His prolonged 
detention adversely affected his family relationships and financial stability, compelling his 
wife and young son to move to her parental home. 
Project Intervention: During a routine prison visit on 21 February 2025, the Prison 
Advocacy Project team identified his case and assessed his legal and social circumstances.

Judge, Banki, on 5 June and 8 July 2025. 
Outcome and Impact: Samir was released on bail on 14 July 2025. The Project 
subsequently facilitated compliance with court requirements and initiated efforts towards 
family reunification and restoration of  his previous employment at a prawn processing 
factory in Chennai. 
Significance: The case demonstrates how persistent legal assistance, combined with 
family-oriented social intervention, can help address the consequences of  prolonged 
detention.

8. Core Components – Stress, Anger, Reintegration 
Stress Management: Deep breathing, meditation, structured routine, physical movement, positive 
thinking, emotional support. Right to healthcare: Article 21 includes health, Prison Act 1894 mandates 
facilities, Nelson Mandela Rules reinforce. If  denied, right to consult doctors, counselling, legal 
intervention via Prison Welfare Officer, legal aid. Anger Management: Types – Passive Silent treatment, 
Aggressive Verbal/physical abuse, Assertive Healthy respectful expression. Legal implications – assault, 
domestic violence. Management – recognition, deep breathing, walking away, journaling, counselling 
(Howells et al., 2005).Reintegration: Educational literacy, vocational livelihood skills entrepreneurship, 
psychological counselling anger trauma recovery, social overcoming stigma rebuilding identity. 
Challenges – discrimination, housing, mental health, employment after release. Former inmates carry 
lifelong stigma. First-time offenders with support could rebuild lives, absence rehabilitation pushes back 
crime. World Bank (2023) each year incarceration reduces lifetime earnings 30-40%.

11. Conclusion 
A humane effective prison system must begin proper classification inmates ensuring individuals not 
further harmed undifferentiated punitive environment. Separation under trial convicted juveniles adults 
casual habitual criminals essential not only maintaining discipline but facilitating meaningful reform. 
Importance engaging prisoners constructive labour safeguarding mental well-being preparing 
reintegration cannot overstated Yet under trial prisoners who form majority continue suffer 
disproportionately due systemic delays procedural shortcomings. Their plight highlights urgent need 
accessible legal support sustained advocacy within correctional system. In this landscape Prison 
Advocacy Project at NLUO collaboration Azim Premji Foundation stands out beacon transformative 
intervention. Ultimately realizing just dignified prison system not responsibility state alone. Calls 
collective commitment government judiciary legal professionals educational institutions society. Must 
strive transform prisons spaces reform opportunity human dignity. If  commitment remains 
unwavering, dream of  constitutional, rehabilitative, equitable prison system India will become not just 
ideal but lived reality – core to Rethinking Crime, Justice & Society in 21st Century.
