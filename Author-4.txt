Abstract 
The news media plays a significant part in informing public opinion through their characterizations of  
different people. With victimization, the news media perpetuates ideas of  who is synonymous with the 
ideal victim, subsequently influencing which missing person cases gain attention in the news media. 
Framed within theories of  implicit bias and intersectionality, the research investigates if  the differential 
demographic makeups between victims’ impact how the news media reports on the story and which 
stories are deemed important. Through the utilization of  critical discourse analysis and content analysis, 
it was found that the news media is significantly more likely to both report on cases of  missing white 
women when compared with missing Indigenous women as well as more likely to blame Indigenous 
women for their victimization. 
Keywords: News Media Reporting, Biases, Mixed-Methods, Content Analysis, Discourse Analysis, 
Missing White Woman Syndrome, MMIWG, Crime Victims 
1. Introduction 
The news media is any organization that gathers, writes, and disseminates online mass communication 
about current events, including online news outlets and social media platforms. This accounts for both 
officially recognized online and social media outlets, in addition to more informal news media groups. 
The news media plays a crucial role in shaping public discourse and opinion. Through their 
characterizations of  different people, they can create and reinforce stereotypes. With this, an 
investigation is needed into the power wielded by media institutions and the role they play in 
perpetuating hierarchical depictions of  different bodies. Like every institution, society built the media 
within a racialized, gendered, and colonial framework. As such, they reinforce specific notions. These 
narratives work to reproduce the systems of  power in which they thrive. Thus, through an investigation 
into Missing White Woman Syndrome and the Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls 
epidemic, the origins and evolution of  this hierarchy will be explored. This will be further explored 
through a critical discourse and content analysis of  the prevalence of, and vocabulary used within white 
versus Indigenous stories. Ultimately, the media perpetuates specific notions of  white versus 
Indigenous women and, as such, works to reinforce socially constructed ideas of  race. This reveals that 
society sees white bodies as more valuable than Indigenous bodies. This is what the proposed research 
aims to investigate further through the following research question: How do differential demographics 
between victims impact what missing person cases are prioritized and how the victim is portrayed in 
the news media?

1. Literature Review 
Missing White Woman Syndrome refers to the overabundance of  media coverage dedicated to missing 
person cases involving white women and the correlating lack of  coverage of  missing racialized women 
(Furey et al. 2023, 1541). It also refers to the media’s tunnel-vision focus on young, white, attractive, 
and rich women (Moss 2019, 741; Slakoff  and Fradella 2019, 81). Society views the disappearances of  
white women as an attack on itself  due to how it positions them within a larger social context. This 
pushes the media to prioritize their stories, mobilizing entire societies to look for the person. In 
contrast, the media is less likely to write stories about Indigenous women, and when they do, the stories 
often perpetuate specific stereotypes. They are framed with stigmatizing high-risk narratives that 
associate Indigenous women with sex work and substance abuse to reduce perceived newsworthiness 
and blame the victim (Moeke-Pickering et al. 2018, 57). This paints a picture of  what an appropriate 
victim looks like, normalizing the disappearances of  Indigenous women (Furey et al. 2023, 1542; Razak 
2000, 106; Razak 2016, 298). Even when cases of  Indigenous women going missing reach the media, 
outlets frequently paint them in a negative light and blame them for their disappearances (Razak 2016, 
298). Also, within the coverage, media outlets do not humanize missing racialized women in the same 
way that they humanize white women, leading to the belief  that white women possess more value than 
their racialized counterparts (Razak 2016, 291; Slakoff  and Fradella 2019, 82).  
Based on the longstanding history of  colonialization, Indigenous women and girls have been going 
missing for decades with little to no public acknowledgement. Colonial violence, racism, and 
oppression connect thousands of  missing Indigenous women and girls (Macklin 2021, 1090-91; 
National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls 2019, 4).  Mainstream 
culture largely fails to acknowledge the connected forms of  violence that Indigenous women 
experience. Authorities have conducted no investigation comparable to those for missing white women, 
and the public shows little awareness of  the genocide (Macklin 2021, 1089-90; Simpson 2016, 4). While 
there would be public outrage if  these acts had been committed against white bodies, popular 
narratives continue to portray Indigenous people as lesser humans to justify the minuscule amounts of  
media attention that outlets give to Indigenous women (Alexander 2010, 98; Macklin 2021, 1104; Razak 
2016, 291). The silence surrounding this crisis exemplifies Judith Butler’s assertion that what is 
speakable in the public sphere is related to who is framed as human and whose deaths are counted as 
grievable (Macklin 2021, 1090). Based on this, decolonial theorists have shown that the category of  the 
human has always failed to account for gendered and racialized bodies, amplifying the intersectional 
nuance of  the issue (Macklin 2021, 1090; National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous 
Women and Girls 2019, 37). 
Despite popular narratives, it is becoming increasingly clear that Canada as a post-colonial state is a 
myth. Since Canadian society was built within coloniality, racism continues to embed itself  within the 
social infrastructure. Race-based ideas and racism persist because these notions built the very structures 
in which Canadians live (National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls 
2019, 4). Based on the desire to separate modern society from its colonial past, many institutions have

The phenomenon of  Missing White Woman Syndrome can be further understood through a case study 
of  Gabby Petito, a young white woman who went missing and was eventually found to be murdered by 
her boyfriend. In 2021, Gabby and her boyfriend were travelling across the United States in their van 
when Gabby disappeared (Furey et al. 2023). She was a low-level influencer who was largely unknown 
to the average person. Yet upon going missing, her online profile grew substantially. The media quickly 
latched onto Gabby’s case, and the story spread around the world in an instant. This not only pushed 
the police to dedicate vast resources to finding her but also motivated the public to take the search into 
their own hands. Gabby’s name spread like wildfire. This prompted a couple who had been camping in 
a similar area to Gabby to search their camera footage (Chang-Yeh 2022, 2). In doing so, they found a 
video of  Gabby’s van, ultimately leading the police to her body (Romano as cited in Chang-Yeh 2022, 
2). As a result, within 8 days of  her disappearance, Gabby’s body was found along with a journal in 
which her boyfriend had written his confession (Furey et al. 2023, 1538). It is due to the vast media 
attention afforded to Gabby that an otherwise unrelated member of  the public was able to play a 
significant role in her case.  
This phenomenon is also evident in the case of  Natalee Holloway, a young white woman who went 
missing in 2005 while on a school trip to Aruba. Leaving the bar one night, Natalee was seen getting 
into a car with a group of  young local men and was never seen again (Tucker 2009, 72). Following her 
disappearance, and for many years after, the media homed in on Natalee’s case. Countless articles were 
released spreading awareness of  Natalee’s case and urging the public to share any information that 
could help in finding her (Tucker 2009, 71). While not all the media attention was positive, there was 
nonetheless ample media attention, so word of  Natalee’s disappearance spread. 20 years later, her body 
has never been found, no one has officially been charged, and Natalee has been declared deceased, yet 
she remains a popular topic for news media and television documentaries (Moss 2019, 746). Online 
chat forums and interactive websites continue to garner attention for Natalee’s case years later as people 
discuss her disappearance with hopes of  solving the case themselves (Tucker 2009, 71-2). Despite the

In contrast to the media attention given to Gabby and Natalee, the cases of  Cindy Gladue and Pamela 
George highlight stark disparities. Cindy Gladue, an Indigenous woman living in Edmonton, went 
missing in 2011. Ultimately, when found, it was concluded that Cindy had been murdered by a white 
male truck driver after paying for her services as a sex worker (Makepeace 2023, 1; Razak 2016, 286). 
Cindy was found having bled to death after the offender left an 11-centimeter wound in her vagina 
(Madigan et al. 2025, 120). Unlike Gabby and Natalee, little media attention was initially afforded to 
Cindy, and it was only after a controversial court even that coverage increased. Even then, the coverage 
overtly blamed Cindy for her victimization and blatantly ignored the dehumanization that she faced 
(Razak 2016, 287-88). Labels such “as ‘native,’ ‘hooker’, and ‘prostitute’” (Madigan et al. 2025, 120) 
were frequently used to justify the offender’s treatment of  Cindy and depict her as lesser. Cindy’s case 
has been studied by many to investigate how colonialism continues to manifest through settlers’ 
possessiveness towards Indigenous women’s bodies. How Cindy was treated via the media shows a 
clearer picture of  the role the media plays in the dehumanization of  Indigenous women.   
In 1995, Pamela George was murdered by two white men in Regina. Pamela was working as a sex 
worker when the two men persuaded her to get into their car (Razack 2000, 92). After driving to an 
isolated field, the men took turns beating Pamela, ultimately leaving her in the mud to die (Razack 2000, 
92). Like Cindy’s case, racial stereotypes propagated narratives around Pamela’s death. These narratives 
drew on the contrast between the space that Pamela came from, a space where violence was regular and 
violations of  bodies routinely occurred, with the space that the offenders came from, one of  wealth, 
whiteness, and privilege (Razack 2000, 93). The media reproduced biased narratives and created further 
stereotypes where ‘Indigenous woman’ was synonymous with ‘sex-worker’, thus validating the 
degradation of  her body. Through this, the media reduced Pamela to a lower-class body that was to be 
paid for, used, and disposed of. This reinforced the offenders’ identities as superior white men.  
The media ignored the intersectional nature of  Cindy and Pamela’s victimization and failed to account 
for the ongoing legacy of  colonialism that impacted their cases. By not contextualizing their 
positionalities as Indigenous women, the media left out the colonial and gendered entitlement that the 
offenders felt towards their bodies (Razak 2016, 292-93). In ignoring both the historical and modern 
contexts surrounding the crimes, the media replicated the colonial narratives that have subjugated, 
dehumanized, and labelled Indigenous women as the other for decades (Makepeace 2023, 2; Razak 
2016, 290; Simpson 2016, 14). The continued lack of  recognition of  missing and murdered Indigenous 
women creates a cycle in which Indigenous bodies are viewed as less important than their white 
counterparts. Society sees Indigenous bodies as disposable, whereas it depicts white bodies as 
invaluable (Furey et al. 2023, 1542; Simpson 2016, 13; Slakoff  and Fradella 2019, 82). When society 
sees them as lesser, the media is not as likely to cover their stories, further perpetuating the idea that 
Indigenous women are not newsworthy. This reinforces a damaging cyclical process. Comprehensively, 
genocidal pasts and colonial presents embed the lives and deaths of  Indigenous women in North

Looking more broadly, this epidemic also poses great barriers to legislative and policy action. Research 
on Missing White Woman syndrome shows how the media influences public consciousness and crime 
control policy (Furey et al. 2023, 1540; Slakoff  and Fradella 2019, 83). The media, as an institution, 
plays a role in depicting what the public should consider a problem. This directly impacts policy. 
Government officials are significantly more likely to implement reactionary policies on issues that the 
public deems important. The common use of  language to paint narratives around white women as 
perfect victims creates a moral panic within a society where the public puts pressure on governments to 
implement policy changes. Moral panics occur when something is framed as a threat to societal values 
and interests (Slakoff  and Fradella 2019, 83). The media, as well as other agents of  social control, 
sensationalize these threats to manipulate what people see as an emergent issue (Slakoff  and Fradella 
2019, 83). With this, the media can control what factors surrounding missing women people deduce to 
be important, thus defining where the panic lies. This provides an important revelation when 
attempting to dissect how institutions define who deserves societal panic.  
3. Methodology 
This research used a mixed-method approach incorporating both qualitative and quantitative methods 
through content analysis and critical discourse analysis. This approach was chosen to utilize the benefits 
of  both qualitative and quantitative research methods. 
First, the quantitative content analysis provided a broad view by looking at what stories news 
algorithms promote. For this, a Google search was conducted with the search words “missing women 
in Canada in 2024 and 2025”. The results of  the search were then looked through. Notes were made 
about the order of  the stories, as well as the number of  stories that addressed missing white versus 
missing Indigenous women. For this, n=35 stories, which covered all the news articles about missing 
women in 2024 and 2025 retrieved by the engine. Next, 5 online news outlets were scanned with the 
search word “missing”. It was then looked at how many of  the top 10 search results were stories of  
missing white women, stories of  missing Indigenous women, or unrelated news stories that had the 
word “missing” in their headlines. Here, n=50, which accounts for the top 10 search results on each of  
the 5 online news outlets used. These news outlets included: CBC, CTV News, City News, Global 
News, and the Vancouver Sun. Next, the official statistics on missing people and homicides in Canada 
in 2024 were pulled to compare the number of  news stories per race with the actual number of  missing 
people. Interestingly, there were barriers to finding data on missing people in Canada that were sorted 
by both race and gender, which will be further discussed later. Quantitative methodology was also used 
to see the number of  articles written about each of  the previously discussed cases. For this, n=830, 
which accounts for all the articles that came up when searches were made for Pamela George, Cindy 
Gladue, Gabby Petito, and Natalee Holloway.  
Next, the qualitative discourse analysis created a focused view on each specific article and how it 
depicted different stories. A comparison between the vocabulary used in stories about white women

4. Limitations 
Overall, the study had some limitations. This was largely due to the use of  secondary research and data 
analysis. By only using pre-existing data, gaps in the research emerged, which made it difficult to 
thoroughly apply the data to the research question. Since the research question was framed within the 
context of  white women and Indigenous women, and the pre-existing data did not break down the 
quantities of  missing women based on race, it was difficult to comprehensively analyze the data. This 
means that some limitations arose when trying to directly compare the number of  news stories with the 
number of  reported missing women based on race.  
There was data on the number of  missing people in Canada in 2024 who were Indigenous. As well, it 
was indicated what percentage of  these missing Indigenous people were women. So, while they did not 
supply specific datasets on what percentage of  the total amount of  missing people were represented by 
Indigenous women, this was roughly calculable by combining the percentages provided. Thus, while the 
use of  secondary data is helpful in getting a general idea of  the statistics on missing people in Canada 
in 2024, the lack of  specificity about how many missing women were Indigenous, white, or other races 
created limitations for the research. It was not feasible to assume that the number of  missing women 
subtracted by the number of  missing Indigenous women equalled the number of  missing white women 
because this would not account for the possibility of  missing women in Canada who were neither white 
nor Indigenous.  
Further, the data supplied by Statistics Canada is not comprehensive, as a vast majority of  missing 
persons cases in Canada go unreported both to the police and by the police to government information 
collection agencies. In fact, according to the National Centre for Missing Persons and Unidentified 
Remains, anywhere from “25%-75% of  missing persons reports made to police are not entered into the 
Canadian Police Information System” (Canada’s Missing 2024, para.12), resulting in discrepancies 
between the number of  missing person cases opened by police and the number reported to the 
government.  
Qualitatively, there are some limitations with the findings due to the number of  articles used. It was not 
possible within the confines of  the project to thoroughly investigate every article written about missing 
women in 2024 and 2025. Furthermore, barriers to finding articles arose because not every article was 
retrieved by the algorithm. This was difficult to get around. Thus, the qualitative findings are not 
comprehensive of  all the articles written across 2024 and 2025 on the subject.

6. Discussion 
Overall, it was found that, similarly to previous literature, there is a stark difference in the words used 
by journalists when writing articles about missing white versus missing Indigenous women. Articles 
about missing white women were more likely to literally inform the public that the person was missing. 
The articles provided key details, like the last time the person was seen. They more frequently asked for 
help, urged people to share a case, and rarely used any words that villainized the missing person. In 
contrast, the discourse analysis revealed the prevalence of  words relating to the missing woman’s 
lifestyle, environment, and life choices when the woman was Indigenous. The articles lacked words that 
depicted the severity and urgency of  the case. They tended to infrequently mention that the woman 
was missing. While this is what previous literature depicted, it is shocking to see not only what words 
were selectively used for articles about Indigenous women, but also which important power words were

7. Conclusion  
Comprehensively, this paper has worked to explore the issue of  Missing White Woman syndrome and 
the impact it has had on societal perceptions of  Indigenous women. It has worked to explore how the 
media reproduces biases of  Indigenous women by differentially assigning humanizing words to white 
versus Indigenous women, as well as writing articles about missing Indigenous women at a lower rate. 
With the growing prevalence of  the media and its continued influence, more people need to be taught 
about its biases. The multifaceted way in which the media can simultaneously perpetuate racist 
narratives while garnering sympathy for certain women demonstrates their influence. Without a proper 
investigation into these echo chambers of  information, the public will continue to go down a path 
where colonial and racialized histories are reproduced. Ultimately, it is through the meticulously chosen 
narratives around missing women that racist stereotypes are reproduced; through this, institutions, like 
the media, work to reinforce both a gendered and a racial hierarchy.
